The Grammar of Connection

Ecology—the science of interconnected systems—wasn't formalized until the 1860s. Yet photographers were already thinking ecologically, capturing not isolated objects but relationships.

A Planetary Consciousness in Different Dialects

In the below graph, each point represents a nationality, positioned by the balance of what they photographed: people, human-built structures, or nature. Hover over points to see exact percentages. Notice how different cultures exhibited different balances between the three categories (revealing different relationships and values), but most have a high proportion of nature.

Now, let's compare different nationalities side by side, through time. Notice what each culture emphasized—and what that reveals about their relationship to environment.

To start you off, notice how for both French and American photographs, the percentage of water photos increased and building photos decreased from 1860 to 1890.

Across 80 years, dozens of nationalities, and thousands of images:
Photographers documented Earth at the turning point—the moment human activity became a planetary force.
They couldn't have known what we know now: that CO₂ would rise from 280 to 420 ppm, that forests would vanish, that their photographs would become evidence in a planetary crisis. But they photographed nature anyway—obsessively, reverently. They were creating an archive of what mattered.
Today, your camera roll continues this archive. Every photograph you take is testimony. What are you documenting? Will your photographs be evidence that we saw what was happening and looked away—or proof that we saw and we acted?
Before you go: Take one photograph today. Not of a vacation or landmark. A living thing in your immediate environment. A tree you pass daily. Water you depend on. Sky you breathe under. Add it to the archive. Bear witness. Then protect what you've photographed.

Dataset

Metropolitan Museum of Art OpenAccess Collection

2,425 photographs from 1839-1920s documenting Earth's transformation during the Anthropocene's beginning. Enriched with semantic segmentation identifying 27 object categories—using computer vision to reveal patterns invisible to individual viewers.
